The Pair: a detector that remembers who it was

Our detector keeps learning from its machine, forever. That is its strength and its one danger: what learns can be mis-taught. The fix costs 473 kilobytes: keep a frozen copy of the detector's own past, and let the two disagree. Every number on this page is a measurement from the experiment record.

ACT I

Two copies of one mind

The live copy learns every day. The frozen copy is the same detector, saved on the day it was born and never changed. A child's version: you can only notice you have changed if you remember who you were.

Why this matters: when a slow fault hides in the training stream, the live copy can quietly learn the fault as “normal” — and nothing about the live copy alone will ever show it. The frozen copy is the outside witness.

ACT III

The repair: subtract the damage

When a model has absorbed a fault, its predictions are pushed a measured distance in the fault's own direction — that is the mechanism (cosine +0.998). The frozen copy tells us the direction. So: subtract it. No retraining. No labels. Drag the slider.

0.32
detection score (0.5 = coin flip)
POISONED
the model right now
0
retraining steps needed

Measured: a poisoned model at 0.27–0.34 (it ranked the failing plant as MORE normal than a healthy one) returns to 0.99–1.00, detecting under drift, with no added false alarms. It requires the shared frozen copy — a sibling machine's model does not work, and on a healthy model this projection gives no benefit. Both limits are measured, not assumed.

What we could not do — measured, kept in the record

The disagreement meter is proven for one fault class so far (faults that shift sensor averages — there it scores AUC 1.000 at every dose we tested, alarms in a median of 9 minutes). For diffuse faults we attacked the problem four separate registered ways and every attempt failed its own pre-set bar; that fault class is covered by our physics and fleet channels instead. Everything here is simulation plus one real water-treatment testbed, three seeds. The next test is a shadow-mode pilot on real plant data. We publish the failures next to the successes; that is why the successes are worth believing.
